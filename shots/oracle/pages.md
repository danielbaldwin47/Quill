# The Lighthouse

The lamp had been lit for an hour before she noticed the boat. It was a small thing, a dark stitch on the water, and it was not moving the way boats move when someone is rowing them. She watched it for a long time. *Nothing out there is ever in a hurry,* her father used to say, and he had been right about that if about little else.

She went down the spiral stair, counting the steps the way she always did, and found the coat on its hook and the lantern beside it. The door took both hands to open. Outside, the wind had the sound of a long vowel.

## What the sea keeps

There are things the sea gives back and things it keeps, and no one has ever found the rule that decides between them. A **bottle**, a shoe, a page from a book — these come home. A name does not.

- the rope, coiled and salt-stiff
- the good knife
- two oranges, for luck

She untied the skiff and pushed off, and the boat that was not moving grew larger in the dark.

It was further out than it had looked from the gallery, the way everything is, and the pull of the tide was a hand under the hull that wanted her south. She rowed across it rather than against it, which is the only argument with the sea that anybody wins. The lamp turned behind her. Every twelve seconds the water ahead went white and then went back to being water, and in the white she could see the boat sitting low, its bow up, nobody in it that she could tell.

She thought about the log book on the desk and the line she had not written yet. *22:35, boat sighted, no oars.* Her mother had kept that book for eleven years and had never once written a sentence in it that could be argued with. The trick, she had said, is to write down what a stranger could check, and leave the rest for the letters you never send.

The oar knocked against something that was not water. She shipped it and sat still and let the tide bring the two hulls together, and when they touched it was a small sound, a knuckle on a door.

## The crossing

There was a coat in the bottom of the boat and a tin, and under the coat there was a dog, and the dog was alive. It looked at her with the flat calm of an animal that has decided the worst has already happened. She said something to it that she did not remember afterwards, and it came to her across the thwart and stood on her feet.

The tin held a spool of line, a stub of pencil, and a card with a name on it in pencil, gone soft at the folds. She did not read the name yet. She made the painter fast, put the dog inside her coat, and started the long pull home with two boats to think about instead of one.

Halfway back the lamp went out.

She counted, because counting is what she had instead of nerve. Twelve seconds, and no white on the water. Twenty. Thirty. The dark that came in was not the ordinary dark of a night with a lamp in it; it was the older kind, the kind the rock had known before anyone thought to put a light on it, and she rowed into it with her jaw set and her hands going numb in their turn.

The clockwork, she thought. The weight is down and the clockwork has stopped and there is nobody up there to wind it, and there is a coaster somewhere north of here making eight knots on a bearing that assumes a light.

She rowed. The dog shivered against her ribs. When the skiff came up hard against the landing she did not tie it properly — she looped it once, which she would be ashamed of later — and went up the steps two at a time with the tin in her teeth.

## The rule that decides

The weight had run down to the floor and the great lens stood still as furniture. She cranked it up, both arms, seventy turns, and the lens took the light and began again to give it away in pieces: white, then water, then white.

Afterwards, when her hands stopped shaking, she opened the log and wrote what a stranger could check.

- 22:35, boat sighted, no oars, bearing NNE
- 23:10, boat taken in tow, one dog aboard, no persons
- 23:40, light failed, weight run down
- 23:47, light restored

Then she sat with the card and read the name on it, and put the kettle on the ring for the sake of having something to do while she decided what to say in a letter that somebody, somewhere, was going to have to read twice.

The dog slept under the desk with its chin on her boot. Outside, the sea went on keeping what it keeps and giving back what it gives back, and the lamp turned, and turned, and turned.
